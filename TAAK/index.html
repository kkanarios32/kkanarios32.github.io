Card. Three hardware bounds and two times [6KRX]

  • August 6, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

What three hardware quantities bound the runtime of any algorithm, and what are the two time formulas?

Answer Compute (OPs/s), bandwidth (bytes/s), and total memory (bytes). Everything an accelerator can do is rationed by one of the three, and the first two give the only two times worth computing: \[T_{\mathrm {math}} = \frac {\text {computation FLOPs}}{\text {accelerator FLOPs/s}}, \qquad T_{\mathrm {comms}} = \frac {\text {communication bytes}}{\text {bandwidth (bytes/s)}}\] "Comms" means either HBM \(\leftrightarrow \) compute cores within a chip, or chip \(\leftrightarrow \) chip over ICI / DCN / PCIe. Same formula either way — only the bandwidth in the denominator changes, which is why the same model covers a TPU Architecture memory stall and a Tensor Parallelism all-reduce.

Card. Why the roofline is right within 2x [BPS8]

  • August 6, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

Given \(T_{math}\) and \(T_{comms}\), what are the lower and upper bounds on runtime, and why is the gap at most 2x?

Answer \[\max (T_{\mathrm {math}}, T_{\mathrm {comms}}) \le T \le T_{\mathrm {math}} + T_{\mathrm {comms}}\] The lower bound assumes communication is perfectly overlapped with compute; the upper bound assumes no overlap at all. Since \(T_{\mathrm {math}} + T_{\mathrm {comms}} \le 2 \max (T_{\mathrm {math}}, T_{\mathrm {comms}})\), reasoning only about the max costs you at most \(2\times \) — which is what makes the roofline worth trusting before any code exists. An op is compute-bound when \(T_{\mathrm {math}} > T_{\mathrm {comms}}\): the FLOPs are the ceiling, and full Model FLOP Utilization (MFU) is the thing to chase. It is comms-bound (equivalently memory- or bandwidth-bound) when the reverse holds and the ALUs sit idle waiting on data.

Card. Arithmetic intensity and the ridge point [KVM8]

  • August 6, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

Define arithmetic intensity and derive the test for whether an op is compute-bound.

Answer Arithmetic intensity is FLOPs per byte moved: \[\mathrm {Intensity}(\text {algorithm}) = \frac {\text {computation FLOPs}}{\text {communication bytes}}\] The compute-bound test falls straight out of it: \[T_{\mathrm {math}} > T_{\mathrm {comms}} \iff \frac {\text {FLOPs}}{\text {FLOPs/s}} > \frac {\text {bytes}}{\text {bytes/s}} \iff \frac {\text {FLOPs}}{\text {bytes}} > \frac {\text {FLOPs/s}}{\text {bytes/s}}\] that is, \(\mathrm {Intensity}(\text {algorithm}) > \mathrm {Intensity}(\text {accelerator})\). The hardware's intensity is its ridge point — peak FLOPs/s divided by bandwidth. For the TPU v5e TensorCore (TPU) MXU that is \(1.97 \times 10^{14} / 8.2 \times 10^{11} \approx 240\) FLOPs/byte. Below the ridge you ride the sloped bandwidth roof; above it you sit on the flat peak-FLOPs roof.

Card. The batch size is the intensity [UZ6D]

  • August 6, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

For a bf16 matmul \([B, D] \times [D, F] \to [B, F]\), give the FLOPs, bytes, intensity, and the resulting rule of thumb.

Answer \(\text {FLOPs} = 2BDF\) and \(\text {bytes} = 2BD + 2DF + 2BF\) — both operands loaded, the output written, two bytes apiece — so \[\mathrm {Intensity} = \frac {BDF}{BD + DF + BF}\] With \(B \ll D, F\) the \(DF\) term dominates the denominator and the intensity collapses to \(BDF / DF = B\). The whole rule of thumb is that the batch size is the intensity: a matmul is compute-bound once the per-replica batch exceeds \(240\) tokens on TPU (\(\approx 295\) on H100, from \(9.89 \times 10^{14} / 3.35 \times 10^{12}\)). Three caveats. \(B\) counts tokens, not sequences. The threshold is per replica — sharding scales FLOPs and bandwidth together, so it applies to each copy of the weights (Data parallelism). And tiling changes the byte count once the matmul is large enough that the operands no longer fit.

Card. Constant intensity of a dot product [02R6]

  • August 6, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

Why is a dot product \(x \cdot y\) essentially always comms-bound?

Answer For \(\mathrm {bf16}[N] \times \mathrm {bf16}[N] \to \mathrm {bf16}[1]\) the work is \(N\) multiplies and \(N - 1\) adds while the traffic is \(2N + 2N + 2\) bytes, so \[\mathrm {Intensity} = \frac {2N - 1}{4N + 2} \longrightarrow \frac {1}{2}\] The point is that the intensity is constant in \(N\): there is no size you can grow into to escape it, unlike a matmul whose intensity rises with the batch. Half a FLOP per byte sits far below any ridge point — even the TPU v5p VPU, at roughly \(7 \times 10^{12}\) FLOPs/s per core, wants an intensity near \(3\). Reuse is what buys intensity. Every byte loaded into a matmul feeds many FLOPs; in a dot product it feeds one.

Card. Weight-only int8 halves the compute-bound batch [JVAB]

  • August 6, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

With weights in int8 but activations and compute in bf16, at what batch size does a matmul become compute-bound?

Answer For \(\mathrm {bf16}[B, D] \times \mathrm {int8}[D, F] \to \mathrm {bf16}[B, F]\) the FLOPs are unchanged at \(2BDF\) and still run at the bf16 rate of \(1.97 \times 10^{14}\), but the weights now cost \(DF\) bytes instead of \(2DF\). With \(B\) small the intensity therefore roughly doubles to \(2B\), and \[2B > 240 \implies B > 120\] Half the usual threshold — weight-only int8 quantization is an easy win even on hardware you are not using in int8. Contrast full int8, weights and OPs: the intensity is again \(\approx 2B\), but the ridge point doubles too, to \(3.94 \times 10^{14} / 8.2 \times 10^{11} = 480\), so \(B > 240\) — unchanged. What full int8 buys is wall-clock at a given batch, not a smaller batch.

Card. Per-example weights destroy batching [S5K4]

  • August 6, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

What is the arithmetic intensity of \([B, D] \times [B, D, F] \to [B, F]\) — a separate weight matrix per batch element — and what is the lesson?

Answer In int8, the work is the same \(2BDF\) — these are \(B\) independent \([D] \times [D, F]\) products — but the traffic is \(BD + BDF + BF\), dominated by the \(BDF\) weight term. So \[\mathrm {Intensity} = \frac {2BDF}{BD + BDF + BF} \approx 2\] constant. Batching bought nothing: \(B\) appears in numerator and denominator and cancels, leaving you comms-bound at every batch size. The lesson is that a matmul's intensity comes entirely from amortizing one weight load across many tokens. Give every token its own weights and the amortization disappears — which is why per-example weights, and anything shaped like them, never reach the flat roof.

Card. The sharded-matmul threshold falls on D, not B [EIZJ]

  • August 6, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

Two chips split \(X[B, D]\) and \(Y[D, F]\) along \(D\), each computing a partial sum and exchanging it. When is this compute-bound, and why is the threshold surprising?

Answer Each chip does half the contraction, \(T_{\mathrm {math}} = BDF / 1.97 \times 10^{14}\), then ships its \([B, F]\) partial sum across the interconnect, \(T_{\mathrm {comms}} = 2BF / 4.5 \times 10^{10}\). Compute-bound means \[\frac {BDF}{2BF} = \frac {D}{2} > \frac {1.97 \times 10^{14}}{4.5 \times 10^{10}} = 4377 \implies D > 8755\] The surprise is that the threshold depends on \(D\), not \(B\): the batch scales FLOPs and bytes-sent identically and cancels out. Same roofline machinery as a single-chip matmul, but because the relevant bandwidth is now the network, a different dimension decides whether Tensor Parallelism pays for itself — the contracted dimension has to be large enough to hide the exchange.

Card. VMEM moves the ridge point [D1X1]

  • August 10, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

VMEM vs HBM bandwidth: what is the ratio, and what does it do to the roofline?

Answer VMEM\(\to \)MXU bandwidth is roughly \(22\times \) HBM bandwidth, so the critical arithmetic intensity for peak FLOPs drops from ~240 to ~10–20. Worked, with int8[16384,4096] weights on v5e: FLOPs-bound at \(B > 267\) out of HBM, but only \(B > 11\) out of VMEM — call it 20 in practice, since you cannot spend all of VMEM's bandwidth on weights. The consequence is VMEM prefetch: load the FFW weights during attention and the load disappears behind compute. The limit is capacity — VMEM is tiny, so a layer's weights have to be small or sharded enough to fit with room to spare.

Card. Decode is bounded by the weight read [YK3Y]

  • August 10, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

200B parameters in bf16 across 32 TPU v4p. What is the lower bound on per-step sampling latency?

Answer \[2\ \text {bytes} \times 200\mathrm {e}9 = 400\mathrm {e}9 \text { bytes} \;\longrightarrow \; \frac {12.5\mathrm {e}9 \text { bytes/chip}}{1.23\mathrm {e}12 \text { B/s}} \approx 10\ \mathrm {ms}\] It is a bound because every decode step has to re-read all the parameters from HBM into the MXU, so no step can beat the weight-load time. At small batch it is close to achievable, too: decode is HBM-bandwidth-bound, not FLOPs-bound, which is the whole reason batching pays.

Definition. Matrix Multiply Unit (MXU) [OGCRP]

  • May 22, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

A core component of the TPU architecture responsible for performing matrix multiplication via a weight stationary Systolic array.

  • \(128 \times 128\) — 16,384 multiply accumulate units, each multiplying and adding in one cycle. v6e/Trillium widens the array to \(256 \times 256\), i.e. 65,536.
  • The native op is one bf16[8,128] @ bf16[128,128] -> f32[8,128] every 8 cycles, or about \(5e13\) bf16 FLOPs/s per MXU at 1.5 GHz. A v5e chip has enough MXUs to reach ~\(2e14\).
Remark. The side length is fixed in hardware, so every dimension has to be tiled down to it and any axis shorter than it is padded up to it, wasting the FLOPs spent on the padding. The order is
  1. Tiling the matrix to load from HBM to VMEM.
  2. Compiler tiles from VMEM to \(128 \times 128\) tiles to load into the MXU.

Card. Why the MXU pads everything to 128 [HPSO]

  • August 10, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

What is the native op of one Matrix Multiply Unit (MXU), and why does that force padding to 128?

Answer One bf16[8,128] @ bf16[128,128] -> f32[8,128] every 8 cycles, on a \(128 \times 128\) systolic array — \(256 \times 256\) on v6e/Trillium. The array's side length is fixed in hardware, so any matrix axis shorter than 128 (256 on v6e) is padded up to it, and the FLOPs spent on the padding are simply wasted. With 2–4 MXUs per core, tiling dimensions want to exceed \(128 \times \) the MXU count. Scale: roughly \(5e13\) bf16 FLOPs/s per MXU at 1.5 GHz, so about \(2e14\) per v5e chip. int8 runs ~\(2\times \) faster, int4 ~\(4\times \).

Card. The systolic array fill bubble [R3DP]

  • August 10, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

Sketch the dataflow through a \(128 \times 128\) systolic array (Matrix Multiply Unit (MXU)). Where does the pipeline bubble come from?

Answer A 2D grid of 16,384 ALUs, each doing one multiply-add per cycle.
  • Weights (RHS, \(128 \times 128\)) are pushed down from the top.
  • Activations (LHS, \(8 \times 128\)) stream in from the left.
  • Each cell multiplies its overlapped pair, adds the partial sum arriving from above, and passes the result down.
The bubble: both operands are loaded diagonally, so the array needs a fill period before every cell is busy. Once filled, new LHS/RHS stream in back-to-back with no further bubble — which is why large matrices amortize the fill and small ones do not.

Definition. Vector Processing Unit [OGCRN]

  • May 22, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

A core component of the TPU architecture responsible for elementwise operations.

  • 2D SIMD execution, where each of 1024 sites execute same program in lockstep.
  • Each site contains 4 ALUs, enabling 4096 FLOPs.
  • Fed a 8x128 Vector Register in one cycle.

Card. The VPU is thirty times smaller than the MXU [JFWY]

  • August 10, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

Vector Processing Unit: what is its shape and throughput relative to the Matrix Multiply Unit (MXU), and which reductions are cheap?

Answer Shape \((8\ \text {sublanes}, 128\ \text {lanes})\), with 4 independent ALUs per position and most ops at 1/cycle: \[8 \times 128 \times 4 \times 1.75\mathrm {e}9 \approx 7\mathrm {e}12 \text { FLOPs/s}\] on v5p — about \(30\times \) less than the ~\(2e14\) MXU FLOPs/s per core. VPU work is fp32. Reductions along the sublane axis (the 8) are cheap: an intra-lane shuffle by 4/2/1 plus three pairwise adds. Cross-lane reductions need the separate XLU, which is slow and expensive. Control is skewed the other way: a single scalar core fetches and dispatches everything and can issue only one DMA request per cycle. Efficient, but it blocks data-dependent vectorization.

Card. The three units and the one data path [MZIY]

  • August 10, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

What are the three units inside a TPU TensorCore (TPU), and what is the data path from HBM to compute?

Answer
  • MXU — matrix multiply on a systolic array. Nearly all of the FLOPs.
  • VPU — elementwise ops (ReLU, add, mul) and reductions.
  • VMEM — an on-chip scratchpad in the TensorCore, like L1/L2 but far larger (~128 MiB on v5e) and programmer-controlled rather than a cache.
The path is HBM \(\to \) VMEM \(\to \) VREGs \(\to \) MXU/VPU \(\to \) VMEM \(\to \) HBM. Nothing computes directly out of HBM. All of it is pipelined — the copies overlap with MXU work, so the array never stalls waiting on memory.

Card. Four communication tiers, four budgets [026G]

  • August 10, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

Name the four communication tiers on a TPU, fastest to slowest, with per-chip v5p numbers.

Answer
  1. HBM \(\leftrightarrow \) TensorCore — \(2.8e12\) B/s.
  2. ICI (Inter-chip interconnect, chip to nearest neighbors) — \(9e10\) B/s per axis one-way, across 3 axes on v5p.
  3. PCIe (host to its tray of chips) — \(1.6e10\) B/s, about \(100\times \) slower than HBM.
  4. DCN (host to host) — \(6.25e9\) B/s.
The design rule that falls out: keep the bytes crossing each channel proportional to that channel's speed. Note also that a TPU\(\to \)TPU transfer over DCN pays the whole chain — PCIe, NIC egress, NIC ingress, PCIe again.

Card. Which TPU axes actually wrap around [590O]

  • August 10, 2026
  • Kellen Kanarios

Inter-chip interconnect topology: which generations get which torus, and when do you actually get wraparound links?

Answer
  • 2D torus, 4 neighbors — v2, v3, v5e, v6e. A pod caps at \(16 \times 16\); past that, pods talk over DCN.
  • 3D torus, 6 neighbors — v4, v5p. Superpods are \(16 \times 16 \times 16\) (v4) and \(16 \times 20 \times 28\) (v5p), built from reconfigurable \(4 \times 4 \times 4\) cubes joined by optical links.
Wraparounds exist only on full-cube multiples (\(4 \times 4 \times 4\), \(4 \times 4 \times 8\) — not \(2 \times 2 \times 4\), since the optical switches only exist on a full cube), or on a v5e/v6e axis of exactly 16, so an \(8 \times 16\) slice wraps the long axis only. Losing the wraparound roughly doubles comms time. Links are nearest-neighbor only and bypass the hosts entirely, so distant chips hop through the chips between them — unlike a GPU's switch hierarchy.
