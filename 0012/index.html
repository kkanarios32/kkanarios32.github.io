1. Dynamic Programming

  • November 24, 2024
  • Kellen Kanarios
Remark. Value Iteration as Linear Algebra. During my brief stint at IPAM, I spent a lot of time focused on solving large systems for discretized PDEs. From this, I learned about iterative methods, such as Gauss-Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel. Interestingly, these methods can be used to interpret some of RL's most fundamental algorithms. If we define the following matrices, \[ \begin {align*} R &= \begin {bmatrix} \mathbb {E}[r(s = 1)]\\ \vdots \\ \mathbb {E}[r(s = n)] \end {bmatrix}, \quad P = \begin {bmatrix} P_{\pi }(s'=1 | s=1) & \cdots & P_{\pi }(s'=n | s=1) \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ P_{\pi }(s'=1 | s=n) & \cdots & P_{\pi }(s'=n | s=n) \end {bmatrix}, \\ V_k &= \begin {bmatrix} V_k(s = 1)\\ \vdots \\ V_k(s = n) \end {bmatrix} \end {align*} \] Then we can write the value function \(V_{\pi }\) as the solution to the linear system \[ V_{\pi } = R + PV_{\pi } \iff V_{\pi } = (1 - P)^{-1} R \] This is just inverting a matrix! Updating one state with the canonical update \[ \begin {align*} v_{k + 1}(s) = \sum _{a}\pi (a|s)\sum _{s^{\prime },r}p(s^{\prime },r\,|\,s,a)\Big [r+\gamma v_{k}(s^{\prime })\Big ] \end {align*} \] Is actually just one iteration of Gauss-Seidel!

        Exercise 2.1. How often greedy is chosen with two actions

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        In \(\epsilon \)-greedy action selection with two actions, how often is the greedy action selected?

        Solution

        Assuming \(Q_*(a_1) > Q_*(a_2)\), the greedy action is selected with probability \(1 - \epsilon \), or \(0.5\) when exploration lands on it by chance.

        Exercise 2.2. Which exploration rate wins in the long run

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        On the 10-armed testbed, which value of \(\epsilon \) achieves the highest reward in the long run, and by how much?

        Solution

        \(\epsilon = 0.01\). Expected reward under an \(\epsilon \)-greedy policy that has converged is

        \[ \begin {align*} R^* = \max _{a} q^*(a) + \frac {\epsilon }{n}\sum _{i \neq a}^{n} q^*(i) \end {align*} \]

        so a smaller \(\epsilon \) pays less exploration tax once the right arm is identified. The exception is \(\epsilon = 0\), which comes with no guarantee at all: with zero initialization, a suboptimal arm whose empirical average stays positive is never abandoned. Some \(\epsilon > 0\) is what buys convergence to the optimal arm.

        Exercise 2.4. Weights on the initial estimate under a varying step size

        Derive the weighting of the initial estimate and of each reward when the step-size parameter \(\alpha _n\) varies from step to step.

        Solution

        Unrolling the update as in (2.6),

        Exercise 2.6. The spikes in the optimistic-initialization curve

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        Optimistic initialization produces oscillations and a pronounced early spike in the optimal-action curve. Why?

        Solution

        Every arm on the testbed has support well below \(5\), so an unpulled arm's optimistic estimate beats the empirical estimate of anything already pulled. The early steps are therefore a round robin over all arms, and — with rewards deterministic — the optimal arm keeps being pulled until its estimate falls below the remaining optimistic ones. That alternation between "sweep the untried" and "exploit the best so far" is the oscillation.

        Exercise 2.8. The UCB spike at step 11

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        UCB performance shows a pronounced spike on the 11th step of the 10-armed testbed, then drops. Why?

        Solution

        With \(10\) arms at equal initialization the exploration bonus forces the first \(10\) pulls to be a round robin. On step \(11\) all bonuses are equal again, so the tie is broken by empirical reward and the best-so-far arm is pulled — the spike. It drops immediately after, because that pull shrinks the chosen arm's bonus and hands the next steps to other, possibly worse, arms.

        Exercise 2.9. Soft-max over two actions is the logistic

        • November 24, 2024

        Show that in the two-action case, the soft-max distribution over preferences is the logistic (sigmoid) function used in statistics.

        Solution

        Expand the denominator:

        \[ \begin {align*} \Pr \{A_t = a\} &= \frac {e^{H_t(a)}}{e^{H_t(a)} + e^{H_t(b)}} \\ &= \frac {1}{1 + e^{\frac {H_t(b)}{H_t(a)}}} \\ &= \frac {1}{1 + e^{-\frac {H_t(a)}{H_t(b)}}} \\ \end {align*} \]

        Exercise 2.10. The value of knowing which case you are in

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        A two-armed bandit alternates between two cases with equal probability. In case A, \(q_*(1) = 10\) and \(q_*(2) = 20\); in case B, \(q_*(1) = 90\) and \(q_*(2) = 80\). What is the best expected reward achievable, and how does it change if you are told which case you are in?

        Solution

        Without the case label, the two arms are indistinguishable in expectation:

        \[ \begin {align*} \mathbb {E}[R(1)] &= 0.5 \cdot 10 + 0.5 \cdot 90 = 50, \\ \mathbb {E}[R(2)] &= 0.5 \cdot 20 + 0.5 \cdot 80 = 50 \end {align*} \]

        so the ceiling is \(50\). Told the case, you can condition on it and pick the better arm in each:

        \[ \begin {align*} R^* &= 0.5 \cdot \max _{a}\mathbb {E}[R(a) \mid x = 1] + 0.5 \cdot \max _{a}\mathbb {E}[R(a) \mid x = 2] \\ &= 0.5 \cdot 20 + 0.5 \cdot 90 \\ &= 55 \end {align*} \]

        The \(5\) points of difference are the value of the observation — which is what turns a bandit into an associative (contextual) problem.

        Exercise 3.3. Where to draw the agent-environment boundary

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        Where should the line between agent and environment be drawn — at the steering wheel, at the muscles, at the intent to drive somewhere? Is any level fundamentally preferable?

        Solution

        Draw it where you can already execute the behavior that carries you from one state to another. Fixing the boundary above the level of reliable control means the agent must relearn what it already has; fixing it below means solving a control problem you did not need to solve. This is precisely the premise of hierarchical reinforcement learning, which raises the boundary over time by learning subpolicies and then treating them as primitive.

        Exercise 3.5. Adapting the continuing formulation to episodic tasks

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        Restate the equations of the continuing case so they apply to episodic tasks.

        Solution

        Add a terminal state — some \(t\) adjoined to \(\mathcal {S}\) — and split the normalization condition on whether we have reached it:

        \[ \begin {cases} p(t, 0 \mid s, a) = 1, &\text { if } s = t\\ \sum _{s^{\prime }\in \mathcal {S}}\sum _{r\in \mathcal {R}}p(s^{\prime },r|s,a)=1,\mathrm {~for~all~}s\in \mathcal {S},a\in \mathcal {A}(s), &\text { if } s \neq t \end {cases} \]

        The content of this is just that once you hit a terminal state you can reach nothing else, and accrue nothing further.

        Exercise 3.6. Return under an episodic pole-balancing failure signal

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        Treat pole-balancing as episodic with discounting and a reward of \(-1\) on failure. What is the return at each time?

        Solution

        With \(T\) the length of the episode and the only nonzero reward at the end,

        \[ \begin {align*} G_t &= R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t + 2} + \cdots + \gamma ^{T - {t + 1}} R_T = - \gamma ^{T - {t + 1}} \end {align*} \]

        Exercise 3.7. The maze-runner that never learns to hurry

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        A maze-running agent gets \(+1\) for escaping and \(0\) otherwise, and after many episodes shows no improvement. What is going wrong?

        Solution

        The task has not actually been communicated. Every escaping trajectory earns the same return regardless of how long it took, so the agent has no reason to prefer a fast escape to a slow one. Fix it by paying \(-1\) per timestep, or by discounting so that a reward collected later is worth less.

        Exercise 3.8. Computing returns backwards

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        With \(\gamma = 0.5\) and the reward sequence \(R_1 = -1\), \(R_2 = 2\), \(R_3 = 6\), \(R_4 = 3\), \(R_5 = 2\), what are \(G_0\) through \(G_5\)?

        Solution

        The recursion \(G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1}\) is solved from the back:

        \[ \begin {align*} G_5 &= 0 \\ G_4 &= R_{5} + \gamma G_5 = 2 \\ G_3 &= R_{4} + \gamma G_4 = 4 \\ G_2 &= R_{3} + \gamma G_3 = 8 \\ G_1 &= R_{2} + \gamma G_2 = 6 \\ G_0 &= R_{1} + \gamma G_1 = 2 \end {align*} \]

        Exercise 3.9. Return of an infinite constant reward stream

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        With \(\gamma = 0.9\), a reward of \(2\) followed by an infinite sequence of \(7\)s, what are \(G_1\) and \(G_0\)?

        Solution

        Peel off the leading term and sum the geometric tail:

        \[ \begin {align*} G_0 &= R_{1} + \gamma R_{2} + \gamma ^2 R_{3} + \cdots \\ &= 2 + \sum _{i = 1}^{\infty } \gamma ^{i} R_{i} \\ &= 2 + \gamma \sum _{i = 0}^{\infty } \gamma ^{i} R_{i + 2} \\ &= 2 + \frac {7 \gamma }{1 - \gamma } \\ &= 65 \end {align*} \]

        and

        \[ \begin {align*} G_1 &= R_{2} + \gamma R_{2} + \gamma ^2 R_{3} + \cdots \\ &= 7 + \sum _{i = 1}^{\infty } \gamma ^{i} R_{i} \\ &= 7 + \gamma \sum _{i = 0}^{\infty } \gamma ^{i} R_{i + 3} \\ &= 7 + \frac {7 \gamma }{1 - \gamma } \\ &= 70 \end {align*} \]

        Exercise 3.11. Expected reward in terms of the policy and dynamics

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        If the current state is \(S_t\) and actions are drawn from \(\pi \), what is the expectation of \(R_{t+1}\) in terms of \(\pi \) and the four-argument dynamics \(p\)?

        Solution

        Marginalize over the action, then over the successor and the reward:

        \[ \begin {align*} \mathbb {E}[R_{t + 1} \mid S_{t} = s] = \sum _{a} \pi (a \mid s) \sum _{s'} \sum _{r} p(s', r \mid s, a) \cdot r \end {align*} \]

        Exercise 3.15. Adding a constant to every reward

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        In gridworld the rewards are positive, so what matters is their differences. Show that adding a constant \(c\) to every reward adds a constant \(v_c\) to the value of every state, and give \(v_c\) in terms of \(c\) and \(\gamma \).

        Solution

        Starting from

        \[ \begin {align*} v_{\pi }(s) = \mathbb {E}_{\pi } \left [\sum _{k = 0}^{\infty } \gamma ^{k} R_{t + k + 1} \mid S_t = s\right ] \end {align*} \]

        and shifting every reward by \(c\), the shift factors out of the expectation as its own geometric series:

        \[ \begin {align*} v_{\pi }'(s) &= \mathbb {E}_{\pi } \left [\sum _{k = 0}^{\infty } \gamma ^{k} (R_{t + k + 1} + c) \mid S_t = s\right ] \\ &= \mathbb {E}_{\pi } \left [\sum _{k = 0}^{\infty } \gamma ^{k} R_{t + k + 1}\mid S_t = s\right ] + \sum _{k = 0}^{\infty } \gamma ^{k} c \\ &= v_{\pi }(s) + \frac {c}{1 - \gamma } \end {align*} \]

        so \(v_c = \frac {c}{1 - \gamma }\), the same for every state — and therefore no change to the ordering of policies.

        Exercise 3.16. Why the same shift is not harmless in the episodic case

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        Does the previous exercise's conclusion survive in an episodic task? Give an example.

        Solution

        It does not. The shift is \(c\) summed over the episode's length, so it is not constant across states — \(v_c\) is larger for states from which episodes run longer. In the maze runner, a positive \(c\) therefore rewards dawdling, which is the exact opposite of the intended task.

        Exercise 3.18. The value of a state as an expectation over actions

        Not a numbered exercise but the question the first part of the book leaves you with: review the tabular methods. What are their strengths and weaknesses, and when should each be used?

        Exercise 8.1. Can multi-step bootstrapping match Dyna with a perfect model?

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        The nonplanning method in the maze example is a special case of the \(n\)-step method. Could a multi-step bootstrapping method do as well as a Dyna agent given a perfect model?

        Solution

        Take \(n\) large enough and the first pass is just as good, in the sense that every \((s,a)\) pair on the encountered trajectory gets updated. But Dyna updates from \(Q\)-values that have themselves been updated, so it is not confined to the \(n\)-step trajectory it actually walked. That extra reach is what an \(n\)-step method cannot reproduce, so I do not think it can match Dyna even with a perfect model.

        Exercise 8.5. Dyna-Q+ in stochastic and changing environments

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        How might the tabular Dyna-Q algorithm be modified for stochastic environments? How might it handle environments that change over time?

        Solution

        Replace the deterministic transition entry with an empirical distribution over successor states and rewards. For a changing environment, weight recent observations more heavily when updating that empirical model, e.g.

        \[\hat {R}_{t + 1}(S,A) = \lambda \hat {R}_{t}(S, A) + (1 - \lambda )R_{t + 1}(S, A)\]

        which is exponential recency-weighting — the same instrument used for nonstationary bandits in Chapter 2.

        Exercise 8.6. When skewed transition probabilities favor sample updates

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        Suppose the branching factor \(b\) is large but the transition probabilities are highly skewed, so a few successors are far more likely than the rest. Does this strengthen or weaken the case for sample updates over expected updates?

        Solution

        It strengthens it. Samples concentrate where the probability mass is, so a handful of them recovers most of what the expected update computes — and the expected update pays for the whole tail to get there.

        Exercise 9.1. Tabular methods as linear function approximation

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        Show that tabular methods are a special case of linear function approximation. What are the feature vectors?

        Solution

        Linear function approximation carries two objects, \(\boldsymbol {w}\) and \(\boldsymbol {x}(s)\), updated as

        \[w_{t + 1} = w_{t} + \alpha (r_t + \gamma w_{t}^{\top } x_{t+1} - w_t^{\top } x_t) x_t\]

        The tabular setting is the one where we can afford a coordinate per state, \(x(s) \in \mathbb {R}^{|S|}\). Take the features to be one-hot, \(x : s_i \mapsto \boldsymbol {e}_i\), and identify

        \[r_i = \mathbb {E}_{a \sim \pi , s \sim p}[r(s_i)], \quad w_i = v_{\pi }(s_i)\]

        Substituting,

        \[w_{t + 1} = w_{t} + \alpha (\mathbb {E}_{a \sim \pi , s \sim p}[r(s_i)] + v(s_{t + 1}) - v(s_{t})) \cdot \boldsymbol {e}_i\]

        Since \(\boldsymbol {w}\) is the table of values and \(\boldsymbol {e}_i\) zeroes out everything else, updating the \(i\)th entry is exactly one tabular update to \(v_{\pi }(s_i)\). Generalization across states is precisely what the one-hot encoding refuses to do.

        Exercise 9.2. Counting polynomial features

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        Why does (9.17) define \((n + 1)^k\) distinct features for dimension \(k\)?

        Solution

        Each of the \(k\) state components \(s_j\) carries an exponent \(c_{i,j}\) chosen from \(\{0, 1, \ldots , n\}\) — that is \(n + 1\) options — and the choices are independent, so there are \((n + 1)^k\) distinct exponent vectors, hence that many features.

        Exercise 9.4. Tile shape as a statement about generalization

        • November 24, 2024
        • Kellen Kanarios

        Suppose you believe one of two state dimensions is more likely to have an effect on the value function than the other. What tilings could take advantage of that?

        Solution

        Use anisotropic tiles — long in the direction you want to generalize along, thin in the direction you do not. Reading the two state dimensions as \((x, y)\) coordinates: to make states sharing an \(x\) coordinate resemble one another, tile with tall narrow rectangles. States at the same \(x\) then fall in a common vertical tile and share their features, while any change in \(x\) moves you into disjoint tiles. Tile shape is how a prior about which dimension matters gets stated.

          Exercise 2.1. How often greedy is chosen with two actions

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          In \(\epsilon \)-greedy action selection with two actions, how often is the greedy action selected?

          Solution

          Assuming \(Q_*(a_1) > Q_*(a_2)\), the greedy action is selected with probability \(1 - \epsilon \), or \(0.5\) when exploration lands on it by chance.

          Exercise 2.2. Which exploration rate wins in the long run

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          On the 10-armed testbed, which value of \(\epsilon \) achieves the highest reward in the long run, and by how much?

          Solution

          \(\epsilon = 0.01\). Expected reward under an \(\epsilon \)-greedy policy that has converged is

          \[ \begin {align*} R^* = \max _{a} q^*(a) + \frac {\epsilon }{n}\sum _{i \neq a}^{n} q^*(i) \end {align*} \]

          so a smaller \(\epsilon \) pays less exploration tax once the right arm is identified. The exception is \(\epsilon = 0\), which comes with no guarantee at all: with zero initialization, a suboptimal arm whose empirical average stays positive is never abandoned. Some \(\epsilon > 0\) is what buys convergence to the optimal arm.

          Exercise 2.4. Weights on the initial estimate under a varying step size

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          Derive the weighting of the initial estimate and of each reward when the step-size parameter \(\alpha _n\) varies from step to step.

          Solution

          Unrolling the update as in (2.6),

          \[ \begin {align*} Q_{n + 1} &= Q_n + \alpha _n [R_n - Q_n] \\ &= Q_{n - 1} + \alpha _{n - 1}[R_{n - 1} - Q_{n - 1}] + \alpha _n \Big [R_n - (Q_{n - 1} + \alpha _{n - 1}[R_{n - 1} - Q_{n - 1}])\Big ] \\ &\vdots \\ & = \left (\prod _{i = 1}^{n} (1 - \alpha _{i})\right ) Q_1 + \sum _{i = 1}^{n} \alpha _n \left (\prod _{i = 1}^{n} (1 - \alpha _{n - i})\right )R_i \end {align*} \]

          Exercise 2.6. The spikes in the optimistic-initialization curve

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          Optimistic initialization produces oscillations and a pronounced early spike in the optimal-action curve. Why?

          Solution

          Every arm on the testbed has support well below \(5\), so an unpulled arm's optimistic estimate beats the empirical estimate of anything already pulled. The early steps are therefore a round robin over all arms, and — with rewards deterministic — the optimal arm keeps being pulled until its estimate falls below the remaining optimistic ones. That alternation between "sweep the untried" and "exploit the best so far" is the oscillation.

          Exercise 2.8. The UCB spike at step 11

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          UCB performance shows a pronounced spike on the 11th step of the 10-armed testbed, then drops. Why?

          Solution

          With \(10\) arms at equal initialization the exploration bonus forces the first \(10\) pulls to be a round robin. On step \(11\) all bonuses are equal again, so the tie is broken by empirical reward and the best-so-far arm is pulled — the spike. It drops immediately after, because that pull shrinks the chosen arm's bonus and hands the next steps to other, possibly worse, arms.

          Exercise 2.9. Soft-max over two actions is the logistic

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          Show that in the two-action case, the soft-max distribution over preferences is the logistic (sigmoid) function used in statistics.

          Solution

          Expand the denominator:

          \[ \begin {align*} \Pr \{A_t = a\} &= \frac {e^{H_t(a)}}{e^{H_t(a)} + e^{H_t(b)}} \\ &= \frac {1}{1 + e^{\frac {H_t(b)}{H_t(a)}}} \\ &= \frac {1}{1 + e^{-\frac {H_t(a)}{H_t(b)}}} \\ \end {align*} \]

          Exercise 2.10. The value of knowing which case you are in

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          A two-armed bandit alternates between two cases with equal probability. In case A, \(q_*(1) = 10\) and \(q_*(2) = 20\); in case B, \(q_*(1) = 90\) and \(q_*(2) = 80\). What is the best expected reward achievable, and how does it change if you are told which case you are in?

          Solution

          Without the case label, the two arms are indistinguishable in expectation:

          \[ \begin {align*} \mathbb {E}[R(1)] &= 0.5 \cdot 10 + 0.5 \cdot 90 = 50, \\ \mathbb {E}[R(2)] &= 0.5 \cdot 20 + 0.5 \cdot 80 = 50 \end {align*} \]

          so the ceiling is \(50\). Told the case, you can condition on it and pick the better arm in each:

          \[ \begin {align*} R^* &= 0.5 \cdot \max _{a}\mathbb {E}[R(a) \mid x = 1] + 0.5 \cdot \max _{a}\mathbb {E}[R(a) \mid x = 2] \\ &= 0.5 \cdot 20 + 0.5 \cdot 90 \\ &= 55 \end {align*} \]

          The \(5\) points of difference are the value of the observation — which is what turns a bandit into an associative (contextual) problem.

          Exercise 3.3. Where to draw the agent-environment boundary

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          Where should the line between agent and environment be drawn — at the steering wheel, at the muscles, at the intent to drive somewhere? Is any level fundamentally preferable?

          Solution

          Draw it where you can already execute the behavior that carries you from one state to another. Fixing the boundary above the level of reliable control means the agent must relearn what it already has; fixing it below means solving a control problem you did not need to solve. This is precisely the premise of hierarchical reinforcement learning, which raises the boundary over time by learning subpolicies and then treating them as primitive.

          Exercise 3.5. Adapting the continuing formulation to episodic tasks

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          Restate the equations of the continuing case so they apply to episodic tasks.

          Solution

          Add a terminal state — some \(t\) adjoined to \(\mathcal {S}\) — and split the normalization condition on whether we have reached it:

          \[ \begin {cases} p(t, 0 \mid s, a) = 1, &\text { if } s = t\\ \sum _{s^{\prime }\in \mathcal {S}}\sum _{r\in \mathcal {R}}p(s^{\prime },r|s,a)=1,\mathrm {~for~all~}s\in \mathcal {S},a\in \mathcal {A}(s), &\text { if } s \neq t \end {cases} \]

          The content of this is just that once you hit a terminal state you can reach nothing else, and accrue nothing further.

          Exercise 3.6. Return under an episodic pole-balancing failure signal

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          Treat pole-balancing as episodic with discounting and a reward of \(-1\) on failure. What is the return at each time?

          Solution

          With \(T\) the length of the episode and the only nonzero reward at the end,

          \[ \begin {align*} G_t &= R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t + 2} + \cdots + \gamma ^{T - {t + 1}} R_T = - \gamma ^{T - {t + 1}} \end {align*} \]

          Exercise 3.7. The maze-runner that never learns to hurry

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          A maze-running agent gets \(+1\) for escaping and \(0\) otherwise, and after many episodes shows no improvement. What is going wrong?

          Solution

          The task has not actually been communicated. Every escaping trajectory earns the same return regardless of how long it took, so the agent has no reason to prefer a fast escape to a slow one. Fix it by paying \(-1\) per timestep, or by discounting so that a reward collected later is worth less.

          Exercise 3.8. Computing returns backwards

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          With \(\gamma = 0.5\) and the reward sequence \(R_1 = -1\), \(R_2 = 2\), \(R_3 = 6\), \(R_4 = 3\), \(R_5 = 2\), what are \(G_0\) through \(G_5\)?

          Solution

          The recursion \(G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1}\) is solved from the back:

          \[ \begin {align*} G_5 &= 0 \\ G_4 &= R_{5} + \gamma G_5 = 2 \\ G_3 &= R_{4} + \gamma G_4 = 4 \\ G_2 &= R_{3} + \gamma G_3 = 8 \\ G_1 &= R_{2} + \gamma G_2 = 6 \\ G_0 &= R_{1} + \gamma G_1 = 2 \end {align*} \]

          Exercise 3.9. Return of an infinite constant reward stream

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          With \(\gamma = 0.9\), a reward of \(2\) followed by an infinite sequence of \(7\)s, what are \(G_1\) and \(G_0\)?

          Solution

          Peel off the leading term and sum the geometric tail:

          \[ \begin {align*} G_0 &= R_{1} + \gamma R_{2} + \gamma ^2 R_{3} + \cdots \\ &= 2 + \sum _{i = 1}^{\infty } \gamma ^{i} R_{i} \\ &= 2 + \gamma \sum _{i = 0}^{\infty } \gamma ^{i} R_{i + 2} \\ &= 2 + \frac {7 \gamma }{1 - \gamma } \\ &= 65 \end {align*} \]

          and

          \[ \begin {align*} G_1 &= R_{2} + \gamma R_{2} + \gamma ^2 R_{3} + \cdots \\ &= 7 + \sum _{i = 1}^{\infty } \gamma ^{i} R_{i} \\ &= 7 + \gamma \sum _{i = 0}^{\infty } \gamma ^{i} R_{i + 3} \\ &= 7 + \frac {7 \gamma }{1 - \gamma } \\ &= 70 \end {align*} \]

          Exercise 3.11. Expected reward in terms of the policy and dynamics

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          If the current state is \(S_t\) and actions are drawn from \(\pi \), what is the expectation of \(R_{t+1}\) in terms of \(\pi \) and the four-argument dynamics \(p\)?

          Solution

          Marginalize over the action, then over the successor and the reward:

          \[ \begin {align*} \mathbb {E}[R_{t + 1} \mid S_{t} = s] = \sum _{a} \pi (a \mid s) \sum _{s'} \sum _{r} p(s', r \mid s, a) \cdot r \end {align*} \]

          Exercise 3.15. Adding a constant to every reward

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          In gridworld the rewards are positive, so what matters is their differences. Show that adding a constant \(c\) to every reward adds a constant \(v_c\) to the value of every state, and give \(v_c\) in terms of \(c\) and \(\gamma \).

          Solution

          Starting from

          \[ \begin {align*} v_{\pi }(s) = \mathbb {E}_{\pi } \left [\sum _{k = 0}^{\infty } \gamma ^{k} R_{t + k + 1} \mid S_t = s\right ] \end {align*} \]

          and shifting every reward by \(c\), the shift factors out of the expectation as its own geometric series:

          \[ \begin {align*} v_{\pi }'(s) &= \mathbb {E}_{\pi } \left [\sum _{k = 0}^{\infty } \gamma ^{k} (R_{t + k + 1} + c) \mid S_t = s\right ] \\ &= \mathbb {E}_{\pi } \left [\sum _{k = 0}^{\infty } \gamma ^{k} R_{t + k + 1}\mid S_t = s\right ] + \sum _{k = 0}^{\infty } \gamma ^{k} c \\ &= v_{\pi }(s) + \frac {c}{1 - \gamma } \end {align*} \]

          so \(v_c = \frac {c}{1 - \gamma }\), the same for every state — and therefore no change to the ordering of policies.

          Exercise 3.16. Why the same shift is not harmless in the episodic case

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          Does the previous exercise's conclusion survive in an episodic task? Give an example.

          Solution

          It does not. The shift is \(c\) summed over the episode's length, so it is not constant across states — \(v_c\) is larger for states from which episodes run longer. In the maze runner, a positive \(c\) therefore rewards dawdling, which is the exact opposite of the intended task.

          Exercise 3.18. The value of a state as an expectation over actions

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          The value of a state depends on the values of the actions available in it. Express \(v_{\pi }(s)\) accordingly.

          Solution

          Same identity as Exercise 3.12 — the state's value is its action values averaged under the policy:

          \[v_{\pi }(s) = \mathbb {E}_{\pi }[q_{\pi }(s, a)] = \sum _{a} \pi (a \mid s) q_{\pi }(s, a)\]

          In the gridworld example, with \(\pi \) the equiprobable random policy, what are \(q_{\pi }(11, \mathrm {down})\) and \(q_{\pi }(7, \mathrm {down})\)?

          Solution

          From state \(11\), moving down terminates the episode, so there is no recursion to unroll:

          \[ \begin {align*} q_{\pi }(11, \mathrm {down}) &= -1 \end {align*} \]

          From state \(7\), moving down lands in \(11\) and picks up its value:

          \[ \begin {align*} q_{\pi }(7, \mathrm {down}) &= -1 + v(11) \\ &= -1 - 14 \\ &= -15 \end {align*} \]

          Review: Part I [sutton-review-1]

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          Not a numbered exercise but the question the first part of the book leaves you with: review the tabular methods. What are their strengths and weaknesses, and when should each be used?

          Solution
          Strength Weakness Use case
          Bandits Simplest case. Can isolate exploration problem. No credit assignment. Action only affects the next timestep. Clinical trials. Things without temporally extended outcomes.
          Dynamic Programming Exact solution. Complexity blows up with state and action space. Model-based. Tabular MDPs.
          Monte Carlo Unbiased estimator. Model-free. Extremely high variance. Must wait till end of episode. When interacting with the environment is inexpensive and episodes are short.
          TD methods Model-free. Online. Biased estimator. Environment interaction is cheap but episodes are long or continuing.
          N step Balances the bias-variance tradeoff. Must choose \(n\). Lower variance in continuing environments.
          Dyna Sample efficiency. Model-based. When interacting with the environment is costly — driving, say.

          Exercise 8.1. Can multi-step bootstrapping match Dyna with a perfect model?

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          The nonplanning method in the maze example is a special case of the \(n\)-step method. Could a multi-step bootstrapping method do as well as a Dyna agent given a perfect model?

          Solution

          Take \(n\) large enough and the first pass is just as good, in the sense that every \((s,a)\) pair on the encountered trajectory gets updated. But Dyna updates from \(Q\)-values that have themselves been updated, so it is not confined to the \(n\)-step trajectory it actually walked. That extra reach is what an \(n\)-step method cannot reproduce, so I do not think it can match Dyna even with a perfect model.

          Exercise 8.5. Dyna-Q+ in stochastic and changing environments

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          How might the tabular Dyna-Q algorithm be modified for stochastic environments? How might it handle environments that change over time?

          Solution

          Replace the deterministic transition entry with an empirical distribution over successor states and rewards. For a changing environment, weight recent observations more heavily when updating that empirical model, e.g.

          \[\hat {R}_{t + 1}(S,A) = \lambda \hat {R}_{t}(S, A) + (1 - \lambda )R_{t + 1}(S, A)\]

          which is exponential recency-weighting — the same instrument used for nonstationary bandits in Chapter 2.

          Exercise 8.6. When skewed transition probabilities favor sample updates

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          Suppose the branching factor \(b\) is large but the transition probabilities are highly skewed, so a few successors are far more likely than the rest. Does this strengthen or weaken the case for sample updates over expected updates?

          Solution

          It strengthens it. Samples concentrate where the probability mass is, so a handful of them recovers most of what the expected update computes — and the expected update pays for the whole tail to get there.

          Exercise 9.1. Tabular methods as linear function approximation

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          Show that tabular methods are a special case of linear function approximation. What are the feature vectors?

          Solution

          Linear function approximation carries two objects, \(\boldsymbol {w}\) and \(\boldsymbol {x}(s)\), updated as

          \[w_{t + 1} = w_{t} + \alpha (r_t + \gamma w_{t}^{\top } x_{t+1} - w_t^{\top } x_t) x_t\]

          The tabular setting is the one where we can afford a coordinate per state, \(x(s) \in \mathbb {R}^{|S|}\). Take the features to be one-hot, \(x : s_i \mapsto \boldsymbol {e}_i\), and identify

          \[r_i = \mathbb {E}_{a \sim \pi , s \sim p}[r(s_i)], \quad w_i = v_{\pi }(s_i)\]

          Substituting,

          \[w_{t + 1} = w_{t} + \alpha (\mathbb {E}_{a \sim \pi , s \sim p}[r(s_i)] + v(s_{t + 1}) - v(s_{t})) \cdot \boldsymbol {e}_i\]

          Since \(\boldsymbol {w}\) is the table of values and \(\boldsymbol {e}_i\) zeroes out everything else, updating the \(i\)th entry is exactly one tabular update to \(v_{\pi }(s_i)\). Generalization across states is precisely what the one-hot encoding refuses to do.

          Exercise 9.2. Counting polynomial features

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          Why does (9.17) define \((n + 1)^k\) distinct features for dimension \(k\)?

          Solution

          Each of the \(k\) state components \(s_j\) carries an exponent \(c_{i,j}\) chosen from \(\{0, 1, \ldots , n\}\) — that is \(n + 1\) options — and the choices are independent, so there are \((n + 1)^k\) distinct exponent vectors, hence that many features.

          Exercise 9.4. Tile shape as a statement about generalization

          • November 24, 2024
          • Kellen Kanarios

          Suppose you believe one of two state dimensions is more likely to have an effect on the value function than the other. What tilings could take advantage of that?

          Solution

          Use anisotropic tiles — long in the direction you want to generalize along, thin in the direction you do not. Reading the two state dimensions as \((x, y)\) coordinates: to make states sharing an \(x\) coordinate resemble one another, tile with tall narrow rectangles. States at the same \(x\) then fall in a common vertical tile and share their features, while any change in \(x\) moves you into disjoint tiles. Tile shape is how a prior about which dimension matters gets stated.
